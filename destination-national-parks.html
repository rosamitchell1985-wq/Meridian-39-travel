Destination File · Utah and Arizona

Nine hundred miles,
five parks, one permit

The scenery is the easy part. The heat window, the reservation calendar and the distance between gas stations are what actually shape this trip.

Sandstone buttes on the Utah and Arizona state line, mid-morning in April. Photograph via Unsplash.

Meridian Field GuideIssue No. 14 Portland, Oregon · 08/18/2026Page 1

By Curtis Boyle  ·  Published 01/19/2026  ·  Last verified 08/11/2026

Overview

Utah markets five national parks as the Mighty 5, and the phrase does real damage to trip planning because it implies they are interchangeable stops on one road. They are not. Zion is a narrow canyon you ride a shuttle into. Bryce is a rim you look down from. Capitol Reef is a fold in the earth with an orchard in it. Arches and Canyonlands share a town but almost nothing else.

The loop runs roughly 900 miles from Las Vegas through Zion, Bryce, Capitol Reef, Arches and Canyonlands, and back out through Moab to Salt Lake City. Seven days makes it a driving trip with hiking in it. Ten days makes it a hiking trip with driving in it. Five days is a mistake, and we say that having made it.

What has changed most in the last few years is access. Angels Landing in Zion now requires a lottery permit. Arches ran timed entry through the spring and fall of recent seasons. The Wave in nearby Vermilion Cliffs has a lottery with odds around four percent. None of this is on the road signs, and every season we hear from readers who drove four hours to a trailhead they could not legally enter.

The other structural fact is heat. Between June and early September, midday temperatures in Arches and Canyonlands regularly exceed 100°F with almost no shade. The parks are still worth visiting in summer, but the usable hiking day runs from about 6 a.m. to 10 a.m. and again after 6 p.m. Plan the middle of the day as a museum, a drive or a nap, and the trip works. Plan it as a hike and it does not.

The essentials

Fly into
Las Vegas (LAS) for a Zion-first loop, Salt Lake City (SLC) for an Arches-first loop. Both are served by every major US carrier.
Loop distance
Roughly 900 miles, 18 to 22 hours of driving spread across the week.
Vehicle
Any car is fine for the paved routes. High clearance is needed only for Escalante and a handful of Canyonlands roads.
Park pass
America the Beautiful annual pass, $80, covers all five plus the Grand Canyon. Individual park entry is $30 to $35 per vehicle.
Cell service
Unreliable to absent in Capitol Reef, Escalante and most of Canyonlands. Download offline maps before you leave a town.
Fuel
Fill up in every town. The gap between Torrey and Hanksville is 47 miles with nothing in between.
The desert does not punish inexperience. It punishes optimism, which is a different and much more common condition. Curtis Boyle, contributing editor

Best time to visit

Two clear windows, one difficult season and one that is better than its reputation.

Season by season, average highs in Fahrenheit at Moab elevation
Window Avg high Best for Trade-off
April to late May78°F The best all-round window. Wildflowers, comfortable hiking, all roads open Spring break crowds in late March, and timed entry systems in effect
June to August99°F Long daylight, full ranger programming, dark sky events Dangerous midday heat, afternoon monsoon storms from mid-July
September to late October80°F Second-best window. Cottonwood colour in the canyons, warm days, cold nights Shorter days, and the first snow can close Bryce viewpoints by late October
November to March48°F Snow on red rock at Bryce, no crowds anywhere, half-price lodging Ice on trails, some roads and campgrounds closed, very short days

Our pick, and why

The last two weeks of April, or the last two weeks of September. Daytime highs sit around 78°F to 82°F, which is warm enough for the Narrows and cool enough for Delicate Arch at noon if you must. Nights drop to the low 40s, so you sleep well, and the light in the last hour before sunset is what everybody came for.

Late September also avoids the monsoon window. From mid-July through early September, afternoon thunderstorms build fast and flash floods in slot canyons kill people almost every year. If you are visiting in that window, treat any chance of rain in the forecast as a hard no on slot canyons, including the Zion Narrows.

Where the money actually goes

Lodging in Springdale and Moab is the whole ball game. Both towns have limited inventory pressed up against a park gate, and rates in April and September behave accordingly. Staying twenty minutes further out, in Hurricane instead of Springdale or in Green River instead of Moab, typically saves $60 to $110 a night at the cost of half an hour of driving.

The annual America the Beautiful pass at $80 pays for itself the moment you enter a third park. Buy it at the first gate.

Costs people forget

  • One-way rental drop fee. Las Vegas pickup with Salt Lake City return frequently adds $150 to $300. Price the round trip before assuming the loop is cheaper.
  • Water and a cooler. Budget one gallon per person per day in summer. Buy the cooler on day one; it pays back in three days of groceries.
  • Monument Valley and Antelope Canyon. Both are on Navajo Nation land, both charge separately, and Antelope Canyon requires a guided tour at $65 to $110 per person.
  • Laundry. Seven days of desert hiking with a carry-on means one laundromat stop, which is $6 and ninety minutes.

Three-day sample plan

The Zion and Bryce half of the loop, at a balanced pace for two travelers on the moderate tier. Costs shown are for both people together.

Day 1

Las Vegas to Springdale  ·  162 miles driving

Arrival, and the canyon at the right hour

Land in the morning, collect the car, and be at the Canyon Overlook trailhead for the last two hours of light. The drive is easy interstate the whole way.

  • 10:30 a.m.

    Collect the car, stock the cooler

    Buy the cooler, four gallons of water, breakfast items and trail food at a supermarket in St. George rather than in Springdale, where the same basket costs about 40 percent more.

    Airport to St. George 1 hr 50 min · allow 60 min for the shop · roughly $95

  • 2:30 p.m.

    Check in at Springdale, then the visitor center

    Park at the hotel and use the free town shuttle. Springdale parking is limited and the park lot fills by 8 a.m. in season. Pick up shuttle boarding passes if required.

    50 min drive · allow 75 min · park entry $35 per vehicle, or use the annual pass

  • 5:00 p.m.

    Canyon Overlook Trail

    One mile round trip through the Zion-Mount Carmel tunnel section for the best view-to-effort ratio in the park. Parking is a handful of spaces, which is the entire difficulty.

    20 min drive up the switchbacks · allow 75 min · no additional fee

Estimated day 1 cost $424

Lodging $178 · groceries and meals $152 · car $78 · fuel $16. Park entry covered by the annual pass.

Day 2

Zion Canyon, all day  ·  14 miles of shuttle, 7 miles on foot

Up the canyon on the first shuttle

The private-vehicle ban above Canyon Junction is the reason Zion still works. First shuttle is around 6 a.m. in peak season, and being on it changes the whole day.

  • 6:15 a.m.

    Riverside Walk and the Narrows gateway

    A paved mile to the point where the canyon walls close in. Wade upstream as far as the flow and your footwear allow. Rent neoprene socks and canyon boots in town if the water is under 55°F.

    40 min shuttle to the last stop · allow 240 min · gear rental $32 per person

  • 11:30 a.m.

    Zion Lodge lawn, lunch out of the cooler

    Shade, restrooms, and the only food service inside the canyon. The cottonwoods here are the biggest trees you will see all week.

    25 min shuttle · allow 60 min · roughly $24 if you buy rather than pack

  • 1:00 p.m.

    Emerald Pools, lower and middle

    Two miles round trip with shade for most of it, and a hanging garden with water dripping off the overhang. Skip the upper pool in summer heat.

    5 min walk from the lodge · allow 105 min · no fee

  • 4:30 p.m.

    Pa'rus Trail and the Watchman at golden hour

    Paved, flat, bicycle and wheelchair accessible, running along the Virgin River from the visitor center. The Watchman lights up red about forty minutes before sunset.

    20 min shuttle · allow 90 min · no fee

Estimated day 2 cost $486

Lodging $178 · meals $152 · car $78 · Narrows gear rental $64 · fuel $14.

Day 3

Springdale to Bryce Canyon  ·  86 miles driving

From the canyon floor to the rim

Bryce sits at 8,000 feet and runs about 20°F colder than Zion. Pack a fleece even in July, and expect the sunrise to be genuinely cold.

  • 7:45 a.m.

    Drive the Zion-Mount Carmel Highway

    Switchbacks, a mile-long 1930 tunnel with windows cut into the rock, and the slickrock checkerboard mesa on the far side. Oversized vehicles need a tunnel escort.

    1 hr 50 min drive · allow 120 min with stops · fuel $36

  • 10:30 a.m.

    Bryce Point and Inspiration Point

    Start with the overlooks to understand the shape of the amphitheatre before you descend into it. The rim path between them is level and paved.

    15 min drive from the gate · allow 90 min · annual pass

  • 12:45 p.m.

    Navajo Loop and Queens Garden

    The three-mile combination that drops through Wall Street and climbs back out past the hoodoos. Do it counterclockwise: descend the steep switchbacks, climb the gentle side.

    5 min drive to Sunset Point · allow 180 min · no additional fee

  • 8:30 p.m.

    Ranger astronomy programme

    Bryce is a certified Dark Sky park and one of the darkest in the lower 48. Programmes run several nights a week in summer. Bring the fleece you packed and a red-filter light.

    10 min drive · allow 120 min · free, first come first served

Estimated day 3 cost $444

Lodging $178 · meals $152 · car $78 · fuel $36. Park entry covered by the annual pass.

Getting around

This is a car trip and there is no version of it that is not. Amtrak reaches Green River once a day and there is no meaningful bus network between the parks. Rent from the airport, take the collision waiver seriously, and check whether your policy covers unpaved roads, because several rental agreements exclude them entirely.

Driving distances on the loop

  • Las Vegas to Springdale, Zion: 162 miles, 2 hr 45 min.
  • Springdale to Bryce Canyon: 86 miles, 1 hr 50 min.
  • Bryce to Torrey, Capitol Reef: 111 miles, 2 hr 20 min on Scenic Byway 12.
  • Torrey to Moab: 148 miles, 2 hr 30 min.
  • Moab to Salt Lake City: 234 miles, 3 hr 45 min.

The one road worth planning around

Scenic Byway 12 between Bryce and Torrey is one of the best drives in the United States, including the Hogback, a section with a sheer drop on both sides of the road and no guardrail. Allow four hours rather than two and stop at Kiva Koffeehouse and the Head of the Rocks overlook.

Shuttles

Zion Canyon runs a mandatory shuttle from roughly March to November. Bryce runs an optional shuttle in summer. Everywhere else you drive to the trailhead, and the trailhead lots fill early. In Arches, be through the gate before 8 a.m. or after 4 p.m.

Where to stay

Four bases cover the whole loop. Book Springdale and Moab first; they sell out earliest and price hardest.

Springdale, Utah

Suits
Zion, two or three nights. Walk to the pedestrian park entrance.
Rate
$180 to $420 in season. Hurricane, 25 minutes west, runs $95 to $170.
Note
A free town shuttle runs the length of the main street to the park gate.

Bryce Canyon City or Tropic

Suits
One or two nights for the amphitheatre and the night sky programme.
Rate
$140 to $260. Tropic, 11 miles east, is consistently cheaper and quieter.
Note
At 8,000 feet, nights drop into the 40s even in July.

Torrey, Utah

Suits
Capitol Reef and the eastern end of Scenic Byway 12.
Rate
$120 to $230. A small town with three good restaurants and very dark skies.
Note
Cell service is patchy. Download offline maps before you leave Bryce.

Moab, Utah

Suits
Arches and Canyonlands, two or three nights, plus rafting and jeep operators.
Rate
$165 to $380. Green River, 50 minutes north, is $95 to $150.
Note
The busiest town on the loop, and the only one with a genuine restaurant scene.

Safety notes

Search and rescue call-outs in these parks are overwhelmingly heat, water and navigation. All three are avoidable with planning.

Heat and dehydration

The risk
Above 100°F with no shade and very low humidity, you lose water faster than you notice. Heat illness is the leading cause of rescue in Arches and Canyonlands.
What to do
One gallon per person per day in summer, and add electrolytes. Hike before 10 a.m. and after 6 p.m. If somebody stops sweating or becomes confused, that is an emergency, not a rest stop.

Flash floods

The risk
Rain thirty miles away can fill a slot canyon you are standing in. The Zion Narrows, Buckskin Gulch and the Escalante slots all flood, with fatalities in recent years.
What to do
Check the flash flood potential rating at the visitor center every morning. Any rating above "expected" means stay out. There is no safe version of a slot canyon under a thunderstorm.

Exposure and falls

The risk
Angels Landing and parts of the Devils Garden traverse have long, unprotected drops. Slickrock is genuinely slick when wet or dusty.
What to do
Turn around if the wind is up or the rock is wet. Nobody at the bottom is impressed. Keep children within reach at every overlook, most of which have no railing.

Vehicle and communications

The risk
Long empty stretches, no cell service, and towing costs that start around $400.
What to do
Fill up in every town. Carry extra water in the vehicle, separate from what you hike with. Tell somebody your route. A satellite messenger is genuinely worth renting for the Escalante section.

Wildlife and terrain

Cryptobiotic soil
The black crust beside desert trails is a living organism that takes decades to regrow. Stay on rock or in washes. Signs say "don't bust the crust" and they mean it.
Snakes and scorpions
Present but rarely a problem. Do not put hands into cracks you cannot see into.
Every rescue I have watched started with somebody carrying one bottle of water on a trail that needed four. Curtis Boyle, contributing editor
