Reading the output

How to read a day plan without being ruled by it

The times are anchors, not appointments. The first stop of the day is the only one worth treating as fixed, because it is usually the one where arriving early is the entire point. Everything after it will drift, and the plan is built to absorb that drift with a thirty-five minute buffer between stops.

The cost line is the part most readers find useful. It shows what a given Tuesday will cost your whole party, split into lodging, meals and admissions. If day four is the expensive one, you can see that on the page rather than on your statement three weeks later.

Remove any stop and the day recalculates immediately. If a day still feels overloaded, drop the pace to relaxed and rebuild. Three genuine stops beats five rushed ones on almost every trip we have ever documented.

Finally, print it. Data roaming fails, phones die, and there is a particular kind of calm that comes from a folded sheet of paper in a coat pocket. Pro subscribers get a properly typeset PDF; everyone else can use the browser print dialog, which we have styled for letter and A4.

If the planner is not enough

Three honest alternatives, including one that costs nothing and one that is not us.

Start from a finished itinerary instead

When
You have the destination but no instinct for the shape of the days.
What to do
Read one of the sample itineraries end to end, then use it as your brief. They are free and complete.

Have an editor rebuild it

When
Fixed dates you cannot move, a milestone trip, or logistics that involve more than one country.
What it costs
$149 domestic, $249 international, one time. Five business days. Details on the pricing page.

Use a travel agent

When
Accessibility requirements, large groups, cruises, or anything where somebody needs to be reachable at 2 a.m. from another time zone.
What to do
Find an independent agent. Many of them run their own planning on our white-label plan, which we consider a compliment rather than a conflict.
