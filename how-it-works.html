Overview

A planner that behaves like an editor, not a search engine

Most trip tools are search interfaces wearing a coat. You type a city, they return a ranked list of everything, and the work of turning that list into a Tuesday is left entirely to you. That is the part that takes four evenings and produces the tab situation.

Meridian starts from the opposite end. Our editors maintain a written stop library for every destination we cover. A stop is not a pin on a map. It is a short piece of reporting with a duration, an admission price, a transit note describing how you get there from the previous stop, and a set of tags describing who it suits.

When you submit the planner brief, we filter that library against your interests, order it so the geography makes sense, and lay it against a clock. The result is a page rather than a list. You can then delete anything you do not want, and the clock and the budget both re-flow around the gap.

The output is deliberately plain. There are no scores, no badges and no confidence percentages, because we do not have a defensible way to compute any of them. What we have is a duration, a price and a paragraph written by somebody who went.

What a stop record contains

Duration
Realistic time on site, measured rather than guessed, rounded to the nearest quarter hour.
Admission
Per-person price in US dollars, refreshed each quarter and dated in the destination file.
Transit note
How you arrive from the previous stop, including the line, the walk or the drive time.
Tags
Outdoors, food, history, art, nightlife, family. Used to weight the selection, never to hide anything.
Editor
The named writer who filed it and the date of last verification.

The four steps in detail

Nothing here requires an account until step four, and even then only if you want the plan to still exist tomorrow.

  1. The brief

    Destination, departure date, length in days, party size, pace and budget tier. Dates use the US format, MM/DD/YYYY. Distances are in miles, temperatures in Fahrenheit, and every price on the site is in US dollars regardless of where the trip is.

    Party size matters more than people expect. It changes the number of hotel rooms, which is the single largest line in almost every budget we produce.

  2. The weighting

    You pick from six interest tags. Stops matching at least one of your tags are placed first, in an order that keeps each day geographically coherent. Stops matching none of them are held in reserve and used only if your trip is long enough to exhaust the matches.

    We do not remove anything from the library based on your choices. If you select only food and then scroll to day six, you will still find the cathedral, because six days of eating is not a trip.

  3. The clock

    Relaxed pace gives three stops a day starting at 9 a.m. Balanced gives four. Packed gives five and starts at 8 a.m. Between each stop we insert a 35-minute buffer covering transit, queueing, coffee and the fact that nobody has ever left a museum on schedule.

    The last stop of the day is capped at 9 p.m. If you want a genuinely late night, add it yourself. A tool that plans your 1 a.m. is a tool that does not respect you.

  4. The money

    Each day totals three lines. Admissions are the sum of the stop prices multiplied by your party size. Meals are a per-person daily figure for your chosen budget tier. Lodging is a nightly room rate multiplied by the number of rooms, which we compute as two travelers per room, rounded up.

    Airfare is deliberately excluded, because it varies by an order of magnitude depending on your departure airport and how far ahead you book. Use the budget estimator on the free tools page to add it.

Free and Pro

Where the free tier stops, and why it stops there

The free tier keeps two trips permanently. Not two trips this month, and not two trips for fourteen days. Two trips, saved, indefinitely, with the full day-plan builder and the whole destination library.

Two is not an arbitrary number. It is what the median American traveler actually plans in a year: one longer trip and one long weekend. If that describes you, you should never pay us anything, and we would rather you tell a friend than open a wallet.

Pro exists for the other group. People who plan constantly, people managing trips for a family across three households, and people who want the itinerary as a PDF they can read on a plane with the wifi off. Those are real costs on our side, so they sit behind the subscription.

What upgrading actually changes

  • Unlimited saved trips instead of two.
  • Offline PDF export, laid out for letter and A4 paper.
  • Up to eight collaborators per trip, each able to edit day plans.
  • Price tracking on the flights and hotels you have flagged.
  • Ad-free reading across the whole publication.

Nothing you have already built is taken away if you cancel. Your first two trips stay editable and the rest become read-only, which is the least hostile version of a downgrade we could design.

Four things this tool will not do for you

Stated here rather than discovered later.

It does not book

Why
We are a publication, not a travel agency. We link to operators and, where an affiliate relationship exists, we say so on the link itself. Your contract is with them.

It does not guarantee opening hours

Why
Hours, permits and closures change on short notice, particularly in national parks and at religious sites. Every destination file carries a last-verified date. Check before you drive two hours.

It does not price airfare

Why
A Newark to Keflavik seat can be $310 or $1,240 for the same week. Publishing an average would be worse than publishing nothing.

It does not replace an agent for complex trips

Why
Multi-country trips with tight connections, accessibility requirements or group logistics benefit from a human. Our custom itinerary service covers some of this, and a good agent covers the rest.
The point of a plan is not to be followed. It is to make deviating from it a decision rather than an accident. Dana Okonjo, founding editor
