Destination File · Iceland

A country you can drive
around in a week

Waterfalls, glacier tongues and black beaches inside three hours of the airport, and a Ring Road that makes the rest of it optional rather than difficult.

Vestrahorn from the Stokksnes headland, late in the afternoon in October. Photograph via Unsplash.

By Tove Lindqvist  ·  Published 03/14/2026  ·  Last verified 08/04/2026

Overview

Iceland is roughly the size of Kentucky and contains about 390,000 people, two thirds of whom live within twenty miles of the capital. That distribution is the single most useful fact for planning. Once you clear the Reykjavik ring, you are in a landscape with almost nothing in it, connected by one paved road, Route 1, that runs 828 miles around the entire island.

For a first visit, the honest recommendation is to ignore the Ring Road and drive the south coast instead. Between Reykjavik and Jokulsarlon, a stretch of about 235 miles, you pass three of the country's best waterfalls, a black sand beach with basalt columns, two glacier outlets you can walk on with a guide, and a lagoon full of icebergs. Doing that stretch properly takes four days. Doing the whole ring takes eight, and a great deal of the middle is beautiful but repetitive.

The other thing to understand before you book is the light. In late June the sun barely sets, which sounds delightful and is genuinely disorienting after three days. In late December you get about four and a half usable hours between sunrise and sunset, which is not enough to drive the south coast in a single day. The shoulder months, September, October, April and May, give you eleven to fourteen hours of daylight, half the summer room rates, and a real chance of aurora.

Prices are high and there is no getting around it. Iceland imports almost all of its food. A sit-down dinner for two with a glass of wine each will run $110 to $140. A gas station hot dog, which is a genuine national institution, costs about $5. Budget accordingly, and lean on the swimming pools, which are municipal, everywhere, and cost about nine dollars.

The essentials

Departure airports
Direct from EWR, BOS, JFK, SEA, ORD, DEN and IAD. Flight time 5 to 8 hours.
Entry
US passport valid for three months beyond departure. No visa for stays under 90 days. ETIAS authorization is expected to apply from late 2026; check before you fly.
Currency
Icelandic krona. Cards are accepted everywhere, including public toilets. You do not need cash.
Language
Icelandic. English is close to universal.
Time zone
GMT year round, no daylight saving. That is 4 or 5 hours ahead of the US east coast.
Tipping
Not expected and not practiced. Service is included.
The weather is not a risk factor in Iceland. It is the itinerary, and it changes the plan roughly every other day. Tove Lindqvist, contributing editor

Best time to visit

There is no bad month, but there are months that suit specific ambitions. Pick the one that matches what you actually want to see.

Season by season, with daylight and average highs in Fahrenheit
Window Daylight Avg high Best for Trade-off
June to mid-August 20 to 24 hrs54°F Highlands access, puffins, hiking, every road open Peak prices, full car rentals, no aurora at all
Late August to October 11 to 15 hrs45°F Aurora returns, autumn colour, room rates fall 30 to 45 percent Highland F-roads start closing from mid-September
November to January 4 to 7 hrs36°F Ice caves, longest aurora nights, Christmas markets Very short driving day, storm closures on Route 1
February to April 8 to 14 hrs39°F Ice caves through March, aurora, low crowds Wind is at its worst, some roads still gated
May 17 to 20 hrs49°F Lowest-cost month with full daylight, waterfalls at peak flow Highlands still closed, aurora effectively over

Our pick, and why

The second and third weeks of October. You keep about eleven hours of daylight, which is enough to drive Reykjavik to Vik and stop four times. Lodging is between a third and a half cheaper than July. The aurora forecast is genuinely live again. And the autumn colour on the moss fields around Kirkjubaejarklaustur is one of the few genuinely unphotographable things in the country.

The cost is weather volatility. Expect at least one day where the wind exceeds 45 mph and the sensible move is to sit in a pool rather than open a car door. Build one flexible day into any October trip and it stops being a problem.

Where the money actually goes

Two lines dominate: the car and the food. There is no functional public transport outside the capital region, so the car is not optional unless you are booking day tours, which cost more. Book it the same day you book the flight, because the Icelandic rental fleet is small and sells out entirely in July.

On food, the single highest-leverage decision is buying a cooler bag at the Bonus supermarket on your first afternoon and filling it. Skyr, rye bread, smoked lamb and a bag of apples turn four restaurant lunches into four picnics with better views, and saves a family of four roughly $340 across a week.

Costs people forget

  • Gravel and sand insurance. Standard collision waivers exclude both. Sand damage on the south coast is a real and expensive event. Add roughly $18 a day.
  • Parking fees at major sites. Seljalandsfoss, Skogafoss, Reynisfjara and Thingvellir now charge $6 to $10 per vehicle.
  • Single supplement. Guesthouses price by room, so a solo traveler pays close to the two-person rate.
  • Airport transfer. Keflavik is 30 miles from Reykjavik. The bus is $34 per person each way.

Sample weekly total

Two travelers, 7 days, moderate
$4,165 on the ground, plus airfare. From Boston in October, expect $520 to $780 per round-trip ticket.
Realistic all-in
Roughly $5,400 for two people for a week, booked ten to fourteen weeks ahead.

Three-day sample plan

The south coast, out and back from Reykjavik, at a balanced pace for two travelers on the moderate tier. Every cost below is for both people together.

Day 1

Reykjavik and the Golden Circle  ·  152 miles driving

The classic loop, run backwards

Every tour bus leaves Reykjavik at 9 a.m. and hits Thingvellir first. Drive the loop in reverse and you will have Gullfoss almost to yourself at ten in the morning.

  • 8:15 a.m.

    Collect the car and clear the city

    Pick up at Keflavik or in town, then take Route 1 east. Fill the tank before you leave the capital region; prices rise noticeably once you are past Selfoss.

    Airport to Reykjavik 45 min · allow 45 min for pickup · fuel roughly $92 to fill

  • 10:00 a.m.

    Gullfoss

    Two tiers dropping into a canyon, with a viewing platform above and a path down to the rim. The lower path ices over from November and is closed when it does.

    1 hr 40 min drive from Reykjavik · allow 60 min on site · no admission, parking free

  • 11:20 a.m.

    Geysir geothermal field

    Strokkur goes off every six to ten minutes and soaks anyone standing downwind. The Great Geysir itself has been largely dormant since 2016.

    10 min drive · allow 60 min · free, cafe and restrooms on site

  • 1:30 p.m.

    Thingvellir National Park

    Walk the Almannagja gorge, where the North American and Eurasian plates are visibly pulling apart, and stand at the site of the parliament founded in 930.

    50 min drive · allow 120 min · $6 parking per vehicle

Estimated day 1 cost $472

Lodging $205 · meals $176 · fuel $41 · parking and admissions $50. Car hire billed separately at $95 per day.

Day 2

South coast to Vik  ·  116 miles driving

Three waterfalls and a black beach

The densest stretch of scenery in the country. Nothing today is more than five minutes from the road, which is why it works even when the weather does not.

  • 8:30 a.m.

    Seljalandsfoss

    The one you walk behind. You will be soaked from the knees down within four minutes, so wear a shell and put the phone in a pocket that closes.

    1 hr 50 min drive from Reykjavik · allow 60 min · $6 parking

  • 10:15 a.m.

    Skogafoss and the stairway

    A 200-foot curtain you can stand directly beneath, plus 527 metal steps to a clifftop platform and the start of the Fimmvorduhals trail.

    30 min drive · allow 90 min · $7 parking

  • 12:30 p.m.

    Solheimajokull glacier walk

    A guided crampon walk on the outlet glacier. Guides supply helmets, harnesses and axes. Book at least a week ahead in shoulder season.

    25 min drive · allow 210 min including briefing · $125 per person

  • 4:45 p.m.

    Reynisfjara black sand beach

    Basalt columns, a sea cave and genuinely lethal sneaker waves. Stay well back from the water line and never turn your back on it. People die here most years.

    30 min drive · allow 75 min · $8 parking

Estimated day 2 cost $687

Lodging $205 · meals $176 · fuel $35 · glacier walk $250 for two · parking $21.

Day 3

Vik to Reykjavik, the slow way  ·  138 miles driving

Canyon, village, and a long soak

A shorter driving day with a deliberate gap in the middle, which is where you put the weather contingency if you need it.

  • 9:00 a.m.

    Fjadrargljufur canyon

    A 300-foot mossy gorge with a rim path. Closed for several weeks each spring while the moss recovers, so check the road authority site the night before.

    50 min drive east from Vik · allow 90 min · free

  • 11:15 a.m.

    Vik i Myrdal, wool shop and bakery

    The Icewear factory shop, one very good bakery, and the red-roofed church on the hill above town with the best view of Reynisdrangar.

    45 min drive back west · allow 75 min · roughly $28 for two

  • 1:30 p.m.

    Seljavallalaug pool

    A 1923 concrete pool built into a hillside, fed by a hot spring, free and unstaffed. Fifteen-minute walk from the parking area. Changing rooms are basic in every sense.

    50 min drive · allow 105 min · free, no facilities

  • 5:30 p.m.

    Sky Lagoon, Reykjavik

    Ocean-edge geothermal bathing with a seven-step ritual, quieter and closer to town than the Blue Lagoon. Book a slot; walk-ins are refused on busy evenings.

    1 hr 40 min drive · allow 150 min · $78 per person

Estimated day 3 cost $573

Lodging $205 · meals $176 · fuel $36 · lagoon $156 for two. Two travelers, one room, moderate tier.

Getting around

Rent a car. There is effectively no intercity public transport, the Strætó bus network outside the capital runs a handful of services a day, and taxis between towns are financially absurd. A compact 2WD is sufficient for Route 1 and the entire south coast from May through September.

You need a 4x4 only for the F-roads into the interior, which are legally restricted to four-wheel drive and are closed from roughly mid-September to mid-June. Renting a Land Cruiser to drive the south coast in July is a common and expensive mistake.

Driving rules that catch Americans out

  • Headlights on at all times, day and night, year round. It is the law.
  • Off-road driving is illegal everywhere, with fines that start around $2,800. That includes driving onto moss to park.
  • Speed cameras are frequent and unmarked. The limit is 56 mph on paved rural roads and 50 mph on gravel.
  • Open a car door into the wind and it can be torn off. Rental insurance rarely covers this. Hold the door.
  • Single-lane bridges are common on the south coast. The vehicle closer to the bridge goes first.

Checking conditions

Two websites decide your day: road.is for closures and surface conditions, and vedur.is for wind and weather warnings. Both are in English. A red wind warning means do not drive, not drive carefully. Check both at breakfast, every day.

Where to stay

Four bases that between them cover almost every south coast itinerary. Some lodging links elsewhere on this site are affiliate links and are labeled as such; nothing in this section is.

Reykjavik, 101 postcode

Suits
First and last nights, anyone without a car for part of the trip.
Rate
$150 to $340 per room in shoulder season.
Note
Everything worth walking to is inside a fifteen-minute radius. Paid street parking applies weekdays until 6 p.m.

Hella or Hvolsvollur

Suits
Night one of a south coast run. Puts you 40 minutes ahead of the Reykjavik traffic.
Rate
$120 to $210, mostly guesthouses and farm stays.
Note
Dining is limited to two or three places. Buy groceries before you arrive.

Vik i Myrdal

Suits
The natural midpoint. Everything on day two is within 40 minutes.
Rate
$165 to $290. Books out first in every season.
Note
Small village, roughly 750 residents. One supermarket, closing at 8 p.m.

Hofn or Hali, southeast

Suits
Anyone continuing to Jokulsarlon or going round the full ring.
Rate
$140 to $260. Langoustine is the local speciality and worth the detour.
Note
Four and a half hours from the capital. Do not attempt as a day trip in winter.

Safety notes

Iceland has one of the lowest violent crime rates in the world. Almost every serious incident involving visitors is environmental, and almost all of them are preventable.

Sneaker waves at Reynisfjara

The risk
Waves arriving far higher up the beach than the preceding set, with a strong undertow and freezing water. There have been multiple fatalities, including in calm conditions.
What to do
Read the warning light system at the entrance, stay above the high water mark, and never turn your back on the sea to take a photograph.

Wind

The risk
Gusts above 55 mph are routine in autumn and winter. They flip car doors, blow gravel through windshields and can knock an adult over on exposed ground.
What to do
Check vedur.is every morning. Orange means reconsider the drive. Red means do not drive.

Glaciers and ice caves

The risk
Crevasses are frequently hidden under snow bridges, and unguided ice caves collapse without warning.
What to do
Never walk on a glacier without a certified guide and crampons. Ice cave season runs roughly November to March, and any operator running them in July is running a different, warmer product.

Geothermal areas

The risk
Ground crust near hot springs can be thin. Water in the Geysir field is above 175°F.
What to do
Stay on the marked boards and paths. Keep children within arm's reach. Do not test the water with a hand.

Emergency numbers and services

All emergencies
Dial 112. English is spoken. The 112 Iceland app transmits your GPS location to search and rescue.
Travel insurance
Search and rescue in Iceland is volunteer-run and does not routinely bill, but medical evacuation and hospital care will. US health insurance generally does not travel. Check before you go.
Road closures
road.is, updated continuously, with a colour-coded map.
Almost everything that goes wrong here goes wrong because somebody decided the warning sign was for other people. Icelandic Association for Search and Rescue, public briefing
