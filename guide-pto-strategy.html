The PTO arithmetic

Eighteen days away from a desk, using eleven days of leave. The mechanism is a calendar, not a loophole.

The annual leave calculation, in its natural habitat. Photograph via Unsplash.

Meridian Field GuideIssue No. 14 Portland, Oregon · 08/18/2026Page 1

By Dana Okonjo  ·  Published 08/04/2026  ·  Last verified 08/14/2026  ·  11 min read

The United States is the only high-income country with no statutory minimum paid vacation. What Americans get instead is a negotiated benefit that varies enormously by employer, tenure and industry, and that a large minority of workers do not fully use. Bureau of Labor Statistics data has for years shown private-sector workers averaging around ten days of paid vacation after one year of service and around fifteen after five, with a meaningful share of that going unused at year end.

Given that, the useful question is not how to get more days. It is how to arrange the days you have so that they buy the most continuous time away. And that turns out to be a solvable problem, because the American calendar contains eleven federal holidays, most of which are already Mondays, and because two of them sit adjacent to weekends in a way that produces disproportionate returns.

The basic mechanism

A week of vacation costs five days of leave and buys nine days away, counting the weekends at both ends. That is the baseline every American traveler already knows intuitively. What is less widely used is the effect of stacking a federal holiday against that same block.

Take Memorial Day, which falls on the last Monday in May. Book leave for the four working days after it, Tuesday through Friday, and you have used four days of leave for nine days away: Saturday, Sunday, the holiday Monday, four working days, and the following weekend. The same trick works with Labor Day in early September, Columbus Day in October, and Veterans Day when it lands adjacent to a weekend.

Independence Day is the volatile one. When July 4th falls on a Tuesday or Thursday, a single day of leave bridges it to the weekend and produces four consecutive days off. When it falls on a Wednesday, two days of leave produce five. When it falls on a Saturday or Sunday, most employers observe it on the adjacent Friday or Monday, which produces a three-day weekend and no leverage at all.

The Thanksgiving arbitrage

The single most efficient block in the American calendar is the week of Thanksgiving. Thanksgiving is always the fourth Thursday in November, and the Friday after it is a paid holiday at a substantial share of employers even though it is not a federal holiday. Where that is true, three days of leave, Monday through Wednesday, buy nine days off.

The catch is that everyone else has worked this out. Airfare in the Thanksgiving window runs 30 to 60 percent above the November baseline, and the Wednesday before Thanksgiving is reliably among the busiest travel days of the year at US airports. The arbitrage works best if you fly on Thanksgiving Day itself, which is consistently one of the cheapest and quietest flying days in the entire month, or if you drive.

The December block

Between Christmas Day and New Year's Day there are typically three or four working days depending on the year. Many employers observe both holidays, and a growing number close entirely for the week. Where they do not, three days of leave buy ten days off across the two weekends.

This is the most expensive week of the year to travel almost anywhere in the northern hemisphere, and one of the cheapest in parts of Southeast Asia and South America outside their own peak seasons. It is also, for what it is worth, the one block where colleagues are least likely to need you, which makes the request easier to have approved.

A worked example: eighteen days from eleven

Here is the arrangement we recommend most often to readers, using a calendar year with eleven days of leave available. It produces three separate trips totalling eighteen days away from work.

  • Memorial Day block, 4 days of leave. Nine days away from the Saturday before to the Sunday after. Long enough for Iceland's south coast, the Utah parks, or a genuine European city trip.
  • Independence Day bridge, 1 or 2 days of leave. Four or five days away. A long weekend at a national park, a road trip, or a domestic city.
  • Thanksgiving block, 3 days of leave. Nine days away if your employer gives the Friday. Fly on the Thursday itself and the fare penalty largely disappears.

That is eight or nine days of leave for twenty-two calendar days away from a desk. The remaining two or three days are the ones you should hold back, because the plan above assumes nothing goes wrong, and something always goes wrong.

The red-eye question

A transatlantic red-eye departing at 9 p.m. on a Friday and landing at 9 a.m. on Saturday effectively converts a travel day into a night. On a nine-day block, that is the difference between six full days in Lisbon and seven and a half.

Whether this is a good trade depends entirely on whether you sleep on planes. If you do, the red-eye is close to free time. If you do not, you have exchanged half a day of daylight for a day of exhaustion at the start of the trip, which is a bad trade on a week-long itinerary and a catastrophic one on a long weekend.

Our rule is simple: take the red-eye outbound on trips of seven days or more, and never on trips of four days or fewer. On the return leg, prefer an afternoon departure that lands in the evening, which leaves a full final morning at the destination and gets you home in time to sleep before work.

The part that is not arithmetic

None of this helps if you do not request the leave. The most common reason American vacation days go unused is not that they were badly arranged; it is that the request was never submitted, or was submitted so late that the calendar had filled with other people's requests.

The practical countermeasure is to submit the whole year in January. Not to plan the whole year, which is unnecessary, but to reserve the blocks. Managers approve a Memorial Day request submitted in January almost without thinking about it, and the same request in April is a negotiation.

The second countermeasure is to book something non-refundable early. A hotel deposit is a commitment device, and it is remarkable how much more firmly people hold a boundary they have already paid $180 for.

US federal holidays

Fixed date
New Year's Day, Juneteenth, Independence Day, Veterans Day, Christmas Day. These move against the weekday each year, which is where the leverage varies.
Fixed weekday
Martin Luther King Jr. Day, Presidents Day, Memorial Day, Labor Day, Columbus Day, Thanksgiving. All Mondays except Thanksgiving, which is a Thursday.
Not a federal holiday
The Friday after Thanksgiving, Christmas Eve and New Year's Eve. Many private employers observe them anyway. Check your handbook rather than assuming.

The leverage table

What each holiday block costs in leave and returns in calendar days. Verify your own employer's observed holidays; the Friday after Thanksgiving in particular is not universal.

The ratio column is a blunt instrument. A 3.0x return in a week when airfare is 50 percent above baseline can cost more in dollars than a 1.8x return in a shoulder week. The right answer is usually to take the high-ratio blocks for domestic trips, where you can drive, and the ordinary weeks for international trips, where the fare dominates the budget.
