Destination File · Kyoto

A city that rewards
getting up early

Sixteen hundred temples, one extraordinary market street, and a set of hours in which almost none of it is crowded.

The approach to Hokan-ji through the Yasaka lanes, shortly after the shops close. Photograph via Unsplash.

By Hana Whitfield  ·  Published 02/02/2026  ·  Last verified 07/28/2026

Overview

Kyoto was the imperial capital of Japan for over a thousand years, and it was deliberately spared the firebombing that flattened most Japanese cities in 1945. What survives is not a preserved quarter but a working city of 1.4 million people with an extraordinary quantity of pre-modern architecture distributed through it, sitting in a basin surrounded on three sides by wooded hills.

That geography does most of the planning for you. The famous sites cluster on the eastern hills, Higashiyama, and the western edge, Arashiyama, with the flat commercial centre in between. A well-built day picks one side and stays there. A badly built day crosses the city three times and spends ninety minutes on buses.

The dominant planning problem in Kyoto is not what to see. It is when. Between roughly 10 a.m. and 3 p.m., the top eight sites operate at a density that changes what they are. Fushimi Inari at 7 a.m. is a quiet mountain shrine with a tunnel of vermillion gates. At noon it is a queue. The same building, the same gates, a completely different experience, and the only variable is the hour.

Overtourism is now an openly discussed civic issue here. Local buses on the Higashiyama routes are frequently full, some private lanes in Gion have banned photography with enforced fines, and residents have been vocal about visitor behaviour. Traveling well in Kyoto means going early, using trains over buses where possible, and reading signage that is often posted only in Japanese with a small English line beneath.

The essentials

Departure airports
Fly to Osaka Kansai (KIX). Direct from SFO, LAX, SEA, HNL. Otherwise via Tokyo Haneda plus a 2 hr 15 min Shinkansen.
Airport to city
Haruka limited express, KIX to Kyoto Station, 80 minutes, about $22.
Entry
US passport, no visa for stays up to 90 days. Complete the Visit Japan Web immigration and customs forms online before departure.
Currency
Japanese yen. Cards are widely accepted but small restaurants, shrines and market stalls are frequently cash only. Carry 10,000 yen.
Time zone
JST, 13 hours ahead of the US east coast in summer, 14 in winter.
Tipping
Not practiced. Leaving money on a table causes genuine confusion.
Kyoto is not crowded. Kyoto between ten and three is crowded. Those are different problems, and only one of them has a solution. Hana Whitfield, contributing editor

Best time to visit

Kyoto has two famous seasons and two underrated ones. The famous ones cost roughly double.

Season by season, average highs in Fahrenheit
Window Avg high Best for Trade-off
Late March to mid-April62°F Cherry blossom, evening illuminations, the Philosopher's Path at its best Room rates double or triple. Book eight to ten months out.
May to mid-June76°F Fresh green maples, comfortable walking weather, moderate crowds Golden Week, roughly April 29 to May 5, is a domestic travel peak.
Mid-June to mid-July84°F Rainy season means gardens at their most saturated and low prices Humidity, and rain on roughly half the days.
Late July to August93°F Gion Matsuri in July, river dining platforms, fireworks Genuinely oppressive heat and humidity. Plan indoor afternoons.
Mid-November to early December59°F Maple colour, night illuminations, the most reliable weather of the year Peak pricing again, and the single busiest two weeks in the city.
January to February48°F Lowest prices, temples with almost nobody in them, occasional snow on the roofs Cold, short days, some gardens dormant.

Our pick, and why

The last week of May. You get 76°F days, gardens at full green, evenings warm enough to eat outside on the Kamo river platforms, and room rates roughly 45 percent below the November peak. The maples are famous in autumn, but the moss gardens are better in late spring and almost nobody photographs them.

If you must see blossom, go in the last week of March rather than the first week of April. The bloom window shifts by up to ten days year to year, and the later date carries more risk of arriving after peak.

The Japan Rail Pass question

The nationwide JR Pass rose sharply in price in 2023 and no longer pays for itself on a Kansai-only trip. A seven-day pass costs roughly $350 per person. Kyoto to Osaka and back is about $9. Kyoto to Nara and back is about $12. You would need to add a Tokyo round trip before the arithmetic works.

What does pay is the regional Kansai Thru Pass or simply an IC card, Icoca or Suica, loaded with 5,000 yen. Both work on subways, private railways and buses across the region, and Icoca now works on the Apple and Google wallets.

Costs people forget

  • Luggage forwarding. Sending a suitcase ahead by takkyubin costs $14 to $22 and saves you dragging it up a station stairwell. Almost every hotel arranges it.
  • Coin lockers. $4 to $7 a day at any station, and a genuinely good idea on arrival and departure days.
  • Cash for shrines. Admission booths and offering boxes are cash only, often coins only.
  • Accommodation tax. Kyoto levies a lodging tax of roughly $1.30 to $6.50 per person per night depending on the room rate.

Three-day sample plan

East side, west side, then the wider region. Balanced pace, two travelers, moderate tier. Costs shown are for both people together.

Day 1

Higashiyama, the eastern hills  ·  5.2 miles on foot

Gates at dawn, market at midday, lanes at dusk

Jet lag from the US west coast puts you awake at five whether you plan for it or not. Spend it at the only hour Fushimi Inari is genuinely quiet.

  • 6:30 a.m.

    Fushimi Inari Taisha

    Walk the first thousand gates before the tour groups arrive. Turn back at the Yotsutsuji junction unless you want the full two-hour summit loop, which is a genuine hill climb.

    6 min on the JR Nara Line from Kyoto Station · allow 120 min · free, open 24 hours

  • 9:35 a.m.

    Breakfast at a Kyoto Station counter

    Grilled fish, rice, miso and pickles for about eleven dollars. The second-floor concourse counters open at seven and turn over quickly.

    6 min back on the Nara Line · allow 45 min · $22 for two

  • 11:00 a.m.

    Nishiki Market

    Five blocks of pickles, tamagoyaki, knives and yuba. Eat where you buy it; walking while eating is poor form and several stalls now post signs about it.

    15 min on the Karasuma subway line · allow 90 min · roughly $48 for two

  • 2:05 p.m.

    Kiyomizu-dera and the Sannenzaka lanes

    The wooden stage over the hillside, then downhill through the stone-paved shopping slopes toward Gion. Photography is banned on some private alleys and the fines are enforced.

    20 min on city bus 100 · allow 180 min including the walk down · $8 for two

Estimated day 1 cost $396

Lodging $168 · meals $144 · transport $16 · admissions $8 · incidentals $60.

Day 2

Arashiyama and the northwest  ·  4.1 miles on foot

Bamboo before eight, gold in the afternoon

The bamboo grove is small and photographs badly once it fills. The first train from Kyoto Station gets you there with about forty minutes of quiet.

  • 7:15 a.m.

    Arashiyama bamboo grove

    Shorter than the photographs suggest, about 500 yards end to end. Walk it slowly, then again in the other direction, and you have seen it properly.

    25 min on the JR Sagano Line · allow 60 min · free

  • 8:30 a.m.

    Tenryu-ji temple garden

    A fourteenth-century borrowed-scenery garden that has survived eight fires. Opens at 8:30, which is the earliest garden opening in the city.

    4 min walk · allow 75 min · $14 for two, garden and buildings

  • 10:15 a.m.

    Okochi Sanso Villa

    The hillside garden of a silent-film actor, above the grove and almost always empty. Admission includes matcha and a sweet in the tea house.

    10 min walk uphill · allow 75 min · $16 for two

  • 1:40 p.m.

    Kinkaku-ji, the golden pavilion

    A single fixed loop of about forty minutes with no way to linger, which is the point. Best light is late afternoon in winter and mid-morning in summer.

    35 min on bus 11 then 59 · allow 60 min · $8 for two

Estimated day 2 cost $382

Lodging $168 · meals $144 · transport $32 · admissions $38.

Day 3

Nara day trip and a Pontocho dinner  ·  6.8 miles on foot

Out of the city, then back for the river

Nara is forty-five minutes away and holds the largest wooden building of its kind in the world, plus about 1,200 deer who have learned exactly what a paper bag means.

  • 8:20 a.m.

    Todai-ji and the Great Buddha

    A 49-foot bronze Buddha inside a hall rebuilt in 1709 at two thirds of its original size, which tells you something about the original.

    45 min on the Kintetsu limited express · allow 105 min · $12 for two

  • 10:30 a.m.

    Nara Park and Kasuga Taisha

    Three thousand stone lanterns on the approach, and deer that bow for crackers sold by licensed vendors. Do not feed them anything else.

    20 min walk · allow 120 min · $10 for two

  • 2:20 p.m.

    Return to Kyoto, Philosopher's Path

    A two-mile canal-side walk between Ginkaku-ji and Nanzen-ji, lined with cherry trees. Empty in the late afternoon in every season except April.

    45 min train, then 25 min bus · allow 105 min · free

  • 6:30 p.m.

    Pontocho alley dinner

    A one-lane dining strip along the Kamo river. Many rooms seat six people at most, and a number still decline walk-in foreign visitors, so have your hotel call ahead.

    15 min walk · allow 120 min · $124 for two

Lodging $168 · meals $124 dinner plus $58 daytime · transport $106 for two · admissions $22.

Getting around

Do not rent a car. Parking is scarce and expensive, several key districts restrict vehicles, and the rail network makes driving strictly worse. What you want is an IC card, either Icoca bought at Kyoto Station or Suica added to your phone wallet before you land.

The four systems, in order of usefulness

  • JR lines. Fast, punctual, and the only sensible way to Fushimi Inari, Arashiyama and Nara. Covered by IC card.
  • Subway. Two lines only, Karasuma running north to south and Tozai running east to west. Limited but quick and never full.
  • City buses. Reach the temples the trains do not. Frequently full on the Higashiyama routes between ten and four. Board at the rear, pay at the front on exit.
  • Walking. Higashiyama and central Kyoto are genuinely walkable. Budget 12,000 to 18,000 steps a day and pack shoes accordingly.

Things worth knowing

Taxi doors open and close automatically; do not touch them. Escalators in Kansai are stood on to the right, the opposite of Tokyo. Trains are quiet carriages by convention, so phone calls are not made on board. And Google Maps transit directions in Japan are unusually accurate, including platform numbers and carriage positions.

Where to stay

Four neighbourhoods, each with a different trade-off between price, quiet and walking distance.

Kyoto Station, south

Suits
Day trips to Nara and Osaka, early Shinkansen departures, anyone with heavy luggage.
Rate
$85 to $180. The best business-hotel value in the city.
Note
Not atmospheric. You are staying above a transport hub, and that is the entire point.

Gion and Higashiyama

Suits
Evening walks, early temple starts, anyone who wants the machiya townhouse experience.
Rate
$220 to $520. The most expensive area and the most photographed.
Note
Streets are busy with visitors until about nine. After that it is genuinely quiet.

Kawaramachi and Pontocho

Suits
Food, bars, department stores, the Nishiki market end of town.
Rate
$140 to $310.
Note
The most convenient base overall. Walk to Nishiki, cross one bridge for Gion.

Arashiyama

Suits
A quiet second half of a trip, the riverside ryokan experience.
Rate
$260 to $700 with dinner and breakfast included.
Note
Empties completely after 5 p.m., which is either the appeal or the drawback.

Safety notes

Japan has one of the lowest crime rates of any country we cover. The genuine risks here are heat, earthquakes and accidentally causing offence.

Summer heat

The risk
July and August routinely exceed 93°F with humidity above 70 percent. Heat exhaustion sends visitors to hospital every year.
What to do
Plan temples for the morning and museums or department store basements for 1 p.m. to 4 p.m. Drink more than you think you need; vending machines are everywhere and cost about a dollar.

Earthquakes and typhoons

The risk
Kyoto is inland and comparatively sheltered, but tremors occur and typhoon season runs roughly August to October, occasionally suspending trains.
What to do
Install the free NHK World or Safety Tips app for English-language alerts. In a tremor, stay inside, away from windows, and follow hotel staff instructions.

Etiquette that is actively enforced

Photography
Several private lanes in Gion carry a 10,000 yen fine for photography. Never photograph geiko or maiko without asking.
Temples
Remove shoes where indicated. Many interiors ban photography entirely. Do not enter roped areas of gardens.
Trash
Public bins are rare. Carry your rubbish until you find one, which is what everyone else is doing.

Health and emergency

Emergency numbers
110 for police, 119 for fire and ambulance. English support is available but not instant.
Medical care
Excellent and comparatively affordable, but most clinics expect payment at the time of treatment. Travel insurance is strongly advised.
Pharmacy
Many common US medications, including some cold remedies containing pseudoephedrine, are prohibited. Check before packing.
The rules here are not decoration. They are the reason a thousand-year-old wooden city is still standing. Hana Whitfield, contributing editor
