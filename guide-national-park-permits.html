The permit calendar

Eleven parks that now gate entry, and the exact windows in which the tickets appear. Miss the window and the drive is wasted.

The last mile in, on a trail that still needs nothing but a park pass. Photograph via Unsplash.

Meridian Field GuideIssue No. 14 Portland, Oregon · 08/18/2026Page 1

By Curtis Boyle  ·  Published 07/09/2026  ·  Last verified 08/11/2026  ·  12 min read

In 2019 you could decide on a Thursday night to drive to Arches on Saturday morning, and the worst thing that would happen is you would wait forty minutes at the gate. That is no longer true, and it is no longer true at a growing number of parks. The National Park Service has spent several years introducing reservation systems in response to visitation that outgrew the roads, the parking and the toilets, and the systems are not consistent from park to park.

This is the part of American park travel that most often ruins a trip, because it fails silently. Nobody stops you booking a hotel in Moab for a weekend when Arches timed entry is sold out. You find out at the gate.

What follows is a working map of the reservation landscape as it stood in the 2026 season. Every one of these systems has changed at least once in the last five years and several will change again. Treat this as a guide to the shape of the problem, and confirm the specifics on the National Park Service page for your park and your dates before you book anything non-refundable.

Three different things, often confused

Timed entry gates the vehicle into the park during certain hours. It is cheap, usually a $2 processing fee, and it is about parking capacity rather than trail capacity. Arches, Rocky Mountain and Glacier have all used versions of it.

Lottery permits gate a specific trail or feature where the terrain itself limits how many people can safely be present. Angels Landing in Zion and The Wave in Vermilion Cliffs are the two best known. These are genuinely competitive; The Wave has run at odds in the low single digits.

Vehicle or corridor reservations gate a specific road rather than the whole park. The Going-to-the-Sun Road in Glacier and the Moraine Lake corridor in Banff, which is Canadian but functions identically, are both examples. You can enter the park without one; you simply cannot drive that road.

A single trip can require all three. Our seven-day Utah itinerary touches two of them, and the difference between a good week and a frustrating one is entirely whether you handled them in the right order, months apart.

The two release patterns

Almost every system in the country now uses one of two patterns, and understanding which one applies is more useful than memorizing dates.

The advance block release puts a batch of tickets on sale on a fixed date, typically one to three months before the travel window, at a fixed time, almost always 8 a.m. or 10 a.m. Mountain Time. Popular dates go in minutes. You need to be logged into Recreation.gov with your payment method saved before the release, not at the release.

The day-before release holds back a portion of inventory and puts it out the previous evening, commonly at 5 p.m. or 7 p.m. local time. This is the mechanism most people do not know about, and it is the reason a sold-out park is frequently not actually sold out. If you missed the advance window, set an alarm for the day-before release rather than giving up.

What the lotteries actually mean

A lottery is not a queue. Entering earlier does not improve your odds, and refreshing the page does nothing. What improves your odds is flexibility: most lotteries let you list multiple acceptable dates on a single application, and each additional date meaningfully raises your chance of drawing something.

For Angels Landing, the seasonal lottery runs quarterly with a separate day-before lottery for the following day. The day-before lottery has substantially better odds than most people assume, particularly midweek and outside the spring and fall peaks.

For The Wave, the four-month advance lottery and the daily geofenced lottery are effectively different competitions. The daily one requires you to be physically inside a defined area near the trailhead when you apply, which means committing to being in Kanab regardless of the outcome. Plan a trip that is good even if you lose, because you probably will.

What happens if you arrive without one

At a timed-entry park, you will be turned around at the entrance station and directed to come back outside the reservation hours, which typically means before 7 a.m. or after 4 p.m. That is an inconvenience rather than a disaster, and the early option is frequently the better visit anyway.

At a lottery-permit trail, a ranger will check permits at the start of the restricted section and turn you back. Fines have been issued. There is no negotiating this, and attempting it is a poor use of a ranger's morning.

At a corridor-reservation road, you will be stopped at a checkpoint. In Glacier, hikers with a shuttle ticket or a lodging reservation inside the corridor are exempt, which is a genuinely useful workaround. In Banff, the Moraine Lake road is closed to private vehicles entirely and the only options are the Parks Canada shuttle, a commercial shuttle or a very long bike ride.

The parks that still need nothing

It is worth stating plainly, because permit anxiety causes people to skip trips they could simply drive to. Bryce Canyon, Capitol Reef, Canyonlands, Grand Teton, Olympic, Great Smoky Mountains, Joshua Tree, Death Valley, Badlands, Big Bend and the great majority of the 63 national parks require nothing beyond an entrance fee or the $80 America the Beautiful annual pass.

Even inside the parks that do gate entry, most of the park usually does not. Zion's Kolob Canyons section has no shuttle and no permit. Arches outside the timed-entry hours is the same park with better light. The systems exist to spread visitation, and using them as intended is not a compromise.

A practical sequence

Six months out, identify which of your parks gate anything. Four months out, enter any lotteries, listing every date you could accept. Three months out, book the timed entry the day it releases, with a saved payment method and an alarm. Two weeks out, check for cancellations, which are common. The night before, check the day-before release at exactly the published time.

And build the trip so that losing any single permit costs you a morning rather than the week. Every itinerary we publish is constructed that way, which is why none of them require Angels Landing.

Where to check, and only here

Recreation.gov
The federal booking platform for almost every permit, timed entry and campground in the system.
nps.gov, the park's own page
The authoritative source for whether a system is in effect for your dates. Systems are announced and cancelled here first.
Not third-party sites
Several commercial sites charge a fee to enter free lotteries on your behalf. They have no advantage and no special access.
Build the trip so that losing any single permit costs you a morning, not the week. Curtis Boyle, contributing editor

The two alarms to set

Advance release
Fixed date, usually 8 or 10 a.m. Mountain Time. Be logged in five minutes early with payment saved.
Day-before release
Commonly 5 or 7 p.m. local time the previous evening. Far less competitive than the advance window.
