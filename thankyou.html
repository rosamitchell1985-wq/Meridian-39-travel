From the editors

The planning is the trip, whether you admit it or not

The average American worker takes eleven days of paid time off a year and spends a startling share of them in an airport, on a shuttle bus, or standing outside a museum that closed forty minutes ago. That is not a failure of enthusiasm. It is a failure of documentation. Most trips are planned across eight browser tabs, three screenshots, a note app, one group chat and a rental car confirmation nobody can find on the morning of departure.

Meridian Field Guide exists because a trip plan is a document, and documents deserve to be typeset properly. You tell us where you are going, how many days you have and what you actually enjoy. We lay out each day as a page: stops with real clock times, the travel time between them, the admission price of each, and a running cost for the day in US dollars.

Nothing here is generated from nowhere. Every stop in our libraries has been walked, driven or eaten by an editor or a contributing writer, and every entry carries the practical detail that only shows up in person. Which trailhead has parking at 7 a.m. Which museum needs a timed ticket booked eleven days out. Which waterfall will soak you to the knee if you approach from the left.

We are a small, independent publication with a software habit. That combination shapes what we build. The planner is free for your first two trips because most people take one or two real trips a year, and charging them for it would be rude. Travelers who plan constantly pay for Pro. Some hotels and tour operators pay us a commission when a booking starts here, and we say so on the page where it happens, every time.

A plan is not a cage. It is the reason you have time to sit down for two hours in the afternoon. Dana Okonjo, founding editor

Four steps, about nine minutes

The planner is a genuine ordered process, so we have numbered it. Each step feeds the next one, and you can go back and change any of them without losing the days you have already built.

  1. Describe the trip in plain terms

    Destination, departure date in MM/DD/YYYY, number of days, how many people are coming, and whether you want a relaxed, balanced or packed pace. Nine fields. No account required to start.

  2. Tell us what you actually like

    Outdoors, food, history, art, nightlife, traveling with children. The planner weights the stop library toward what you picked instead of handing everyone the same top ten list.

  3. Read the day plans and cut what you do not want

    Every day arrives as a typeset page with time-stamped stops, transit notes between them and a daily cost. Remove a stop and the clock and the budget both recalculate.

  4. Save it, print it, or hand it to a human

    Free accounts keep two trips. Pro adds unlimited trips, offline PDF export and collaborator access. If you want an editor to review the whole thing, our custom itinerary service is a one-time fee.

A specimen page

This is what the planner prints for a balanced-pace first day in Kyoto, for two travelers on a moderate budget. Not a mock-up. It is the same layout the tool produces.

Day 1  ·  10/06/2026

Balanced pace  ·  4 stops  ·  5.2 miles on foot

Kyoto: east bank, temple to market

Jet lag is an asset on day one in Japan. You will be awake at five whether you like it or not, so we spend it at the only hour Fushimi Inari is quiet.

  • 6:30 a.m.

    Fushimi Inari Taisha, lower gates

    Walk the first thousand torii before the tour groups arrive at eight. Turn back at the Yotsutsuji junction unless you want the full two-hour summit loop.

    6 min on the JR Nara Line from Kyoto Station  ·  allow 120 min on site  ·  no admission charge

  • 9:35 a.m.

    Breakfast at a Kyoto Station standing counter

    Grilled fish, rice, miso and pickles for about nine dollars. The second-floor concourse counters open at seven and turn over fast.

    6 min back on the Nara Line  ·  allow 45 min  ·  $11 per person

  • 11:00 a.m.

    Nishiki Market, end to end

    Five blocks of pickles, tamagoyaki, knives and yuba. Eat where you buy it. Walking while eating is considered poor form here.

    15 min on the Karasuma subway line  ·  allow 90 min  ·  $24 per person

  • 2:05 p.m.

    Kiyomizu-dera and the Sannenzaka lanes

    The wooden stage over the hillside, then downhill through the stone-paved shopping slopes. Photography is restricted on the private side alleys, and the signs mean it.

    20 min by city bus 100  ·  allow 105 min on site  ·  $4 per person

Estimated day 1 cost $422

Lodging $168  ·  meals $144  ·  admissions $78  ·  local transit $32. Two travelers, one room, moderate tier.

What the planner does not do

Book anything
We link out. Your reservation is between you and the hotel, airline or operator.
Invent stops
Every entry is written by a named editor or contributor who has been there.
Hide the money
Costs are shown per day and per traveler before you commit to anything.

From the destination files

Sixteen destinations are fully written up, each with a budget breakdown, a three-day sample plan, transport notes and a frank safety section. Four of them below.

  • Iceland7 to 10 days$210 per day

    Iceland, and the argument for going in October

    Shoulder season halves the lodging rate, keeps the Ring Road open and puts the aurora back on the table. What you trade is daylight, which matters more than most first-timers expect.

    Read the Iceland file

  • Kyoto, Japan6 to 9 days$185 per day

    Kyoto without the queue, which is mostly a question of hours

    The city has roughly 1,600 temples and four that everyone photographs. Reorder your day around the 6 a.m. and 4 p.m. windows and Kyoto becomes a different, much quieter city.

    Read the Kyoto file

  • Utah and Arizona7 to 12 days$168 per day

    The national parks road trip, and the permits nobody mentions

    Five Utah parks in a week is achievable. Doing it without an Angels Landing permit, a Moraine-style shuttle reservation and a plan for the 4 p.m. thunderstorm window is not.

    Read the parks file

    Banff, where the hard part is the parking lot

    Private vehicles no longer reach Moraine Lake. The shuttle system rewards people who booked in March and quietly punishes everyone else, so the calendar matters more than the map.

How we make money, in plain English

You are entitled to know who pays for a recommendation before you act on it. Here is the whole list. There is nothing else.

Pro subscriptions

What it costs
$9.00 per month, or $72.00 per year, in US dollars. The free tier keeps two saved trips forever and is not a trial.
What it pays for
Roughly two thirds of our revenue, and all of the editorial payroll. Subscriber money is why the destination files do not read like advertising.

Affiliate commissions

Where they come from
Hotels, flight search, car rental and tour operators. If you book through a link on this site, we may receive a commission from the seller. Your price does not change.
How we handle them
Affiliate links are labeled at the point of use, not only in the footer. No editor is compensated on booking volume, and no partner has ever been given a placement in a destination file in exchange for payment.

Custom itinerary service

What it is
A one-time fee of $149 for a domestic trip, $249 for international. A named editor reviews your draft, rebuilds the day plans and returns it within five business days.
Who buys it
Mostly people planning a once-a-decade trip with a fixed date they cannot move, such as an anniversary or a graduation.

Advertising, B2B plans and referrals

Display advertising
Clearly labeled ad placements on editorial pages. Never on legal pages, never above the main content of the page, never inside a day plan.
White-label for travel agents
Independent agents and small tour operators license the planner under their own brand from $79 per seat per month.
Optional add-ons
Travel insurance partner referrals and offline PDF export. Both are optional, both are disclosed, and neither is ever bundled into a price without being itemized.
